Pre-Edo Money

700BC-1600

During the Yayoi period (300 BC- 300AD) and Kofun Period (300-710AD) Chinese coinage was in use but not as a common form of currency. During this period rice, cloth and barter was the rule.

The first coins minted in Japan were the silver and copper WADO KAIHO (Wado Era coins) issued in 708 and modeled after the Chinese Tang Dynasty coins.

From 760-958 AD, 11 other coinages were issued but barter was still in common practice.

During the 10th century, the Bureau of the Mint (Chusenshi) was abolished and the use of government coinage was prohibited. For about five centuries, until the late Muromachi (1333-1568), the central government did not mint coins but used imported coinage from China, Korea, Annam and other countries. With an increased usage of these foreign coins, a system of cropland value for the purposes of taxes developed called Kandanka based on the copper unit called KAMMON. But coins were still privately minted and the quality varied tremendously an led to a system of Erizeni, or coin selection, that devalued poor ones and appraised good ones.

Edo Money

1600-1868

In the Edo Period (1600-1868) the Shogunate created the Kinza, gold mint, to mint the OBAN (equals 10 RYO), KOBAN (1 RYO), and ICHIBUKIN (1/4th of 1 RYO). CHOGIN (silver bars) and smaller MAMEITAGIN were minted at the gold mints (GINZA). Zeni Coins were copper, iron or brass with a square hole minted at Zeniza (ZENI Mints). In 1636 AD the mintage called Kan'ei tsuho was decided to become the official Zeni currency, and in the 1670 the older coinage was removed from circulation and the modern system of gold, silver, and Zeni was formalized.

THE RYO

A standard unit of currency developed during the 16th century. The standard gold coin of the Tokugawa Shogunate (1603-1867), the Koban was equal to 1 Ryo. Before this, the Ryo was also a standard of weight to measure gold, silver, medicine ad incense. Thus the weight of a gold equaling 1 Ryo was equal to about 4.5 momme (1 momme=3.75 g)

Kokudaka System

A system of assessing the annual tax base in terms of Koku by the Edo Shogunate. This was the estimating in advance of the annual yield of a farm or property in terms of unpolished rice measured in Koku (1 Koku= 180 liters or 5 US Bushels. This is supposedly enough rice to feed one person for a year. Land values, taxes, Samurai wages and the Daimiyo's wealth was all measured in Koku). This system remained in place until the Land tax reform of 1873-1881. Rice fields were classified in four classifications of annual harvest potential (kokumori) set at 1.5, 1.3, 1.2 and 1.1 Koku for each tan (0.1 hectare or 0.25 acre) of farmland. The collector merely multiplied the kokumori by the number of tan to arrive at the Kukudaka or base tax. This system replaced the Kandaka system of the earlier Kamakura (1185-1333) and Muromachi (1333-1568) period based on imported monies. This system acted as a buffer for farmers in an increasingly money based society.

Modern Money

1871-199?

The Han, or feudal domains, issued papper currency (Hansatsu) around 1661 and in the Meiji Era (1868-1912) over 244 regions had thier own currency. This chaos was removed in 1871 with the institution of the Shinka Jorei (New Currency Regulation) that made the YEN (equal to 1 RYO) the basic unit of currency and replaced all other currency by 1879. The Yen is now the standard unit of currency in Modern Japan.

Money Offerings (saisen)

Money offered at a Shinto Shrine or Buddhist temple in repayment for the fullfillment of a petiton or prayer (Gankake). or the occasion of a visit for worship. This custom is believed to only go back as far as the Edo period, but the custom of offering newly minted coins to Shinto or Buddhist Dieties and gods goes back to ancient times. Before money, this custom was carried out with ohineru, a small bit of rice wrapped in paper.
